# USDA-NIFA AFRI Proposal
## Productivity and Sustainability of Soybean Deficit Irrigation in the US

---

## I. OVERVIEW

### Problem and Opportunity

Soybean (*Glycine max* L.) is a cornerstone commodity crop, occupying over 87 million acres across the United States with an annual production value exceeding $50 billion. However, soybean production faces a critical challenge: climate variability and water scarcity. Between 2012-2023, severe drought affected major soybean-producing regions, resulting in yield losses of 10-30% in affected areas. Climate projections indicate that water stress will intensify in major agricultural regions, particularly in the Great Plains and Western Corn Belt.

Currently, only ~15% of US soybean acreage is irrigated, concentrated primarily in the Western Great Plains and Mid-South. The majority of soybean production depends on rainfed agriculture, leaving farmers vulnerable to drought. Paradoxically, in irrigated regions, full-season irrigation is the norm, often applying 250-300 mm of water annually—more than many soils can retain for crop use.

**Deficit irrigation**—strategically applying less than full crop water requirements while minimizing yield loss—offers a transformative opportunity. This approach can:

1. **Increase productivity per unit water** (water use efficiency)
2. **Extend irrigation capacity** to additional acreage
3. **Reduce pumping costs** and energy consumption
4. **Enhance sustainability** by conserving groundwater resources
5. **Build climate resilience** for vulnerable production regions

### Why This Research Matters

Despite growing research on deficit irrigation in other crops (wheat, cotton, corn), **there is a critical knowledge gap for soybeans**. Published research on soybean deficit irrigation is limited, regionally concentrated, and rarely integrated with process-based predictive models. Farmers and extension professionals lack:

- **Region-specific guidance** on optimal deficit irrigation strategies
- **Predictive tools** to estimate yield-water tradeoffs under variable conditions
- **Evidence-based decision frameworks** for adopting deficit irrigation
- **Understanding of physiological mechanisms** limiting soybean production under water deficit

This proposal addresses these gaps through an **integrated field-modeling approach** that combines:
- Multi-location field experiments documenting yield and physiological responses
- Process-based model improvements grounded in mechanistic understanding
- Practical applications producing farmer-ready decision tools
- Regional vulnerability assessments guiding where deficit irrigation offers greatest benefit

### Innovation and Expected Impact

**Innovation**: Integrating field experiments with process-based modeling is common in crop science; however, the specific application to soybean deficit irrigation—across multiple US regions, with explicit focus on irrigation management decisions—is novel and timely.

**Expected Impact**:
- **Near-term (2-3 years)**: Published data on soybean deficit irrigation response, improved model algorithms, early decision tools
- **Medium-term (3-5 years)**: Farmer-adoption of deficit irrigation strategies in vulnerable regions, water savings of 25-50% on irrigated acreage
- **Long-term (5+ years)**: Broader adoption across soybean-producing regions, measurable water conservation (100s of millions of gallons annually), improved crop productivity and resilience under climate change

**Alignment with USDA-NIFA Priorities**:
- Productivity & competitiveness: Maximize yields under water-limited conditions
- Sustainability: Conserve water while maintaining production
- Climate adaptation: Build resilience for climate variability and change
- Stakeholder engagement: Direct farmer and extension partnerships

## II. RATIONALE AND SIGNIFICANCE

### Current State of Knowledge

#### A. Soybean Water Requirements and Sensitivity

Soybean is classified as a moderately water-demanding crop, requiring 400-600 mm of water (precipitation + irrigation) for optimal growth, depending on region, cultivar, and growing season weather. Water is most critical during:

1. **Reproductive stages (R1-R5)**: Flowering through pod fill (July-August in northern US)
2. **Pod development**: When seed mass accumulation occurs
3. **Grain filling**: Directly determines final yield

Water stress during these stages can reduce seed number and weight, with potential yield penalties of 20-40% if stress occurs during peak reproductive demand. Conversely, soybeans can tolerate moderate water deficits (50% of available water) during vegetative stages (V1-V4) with minimal yield impact.

#### B. Deficit Irrigation Principles

Deficit irrigation is based on the principle that crops have differential water sensitivities across growth stages. By strategically reducing water supply during **less-sensitive periods** while maintaining adequate water during **critical periods**, producers can achieve:

- **Partial deficit irrigation (PDI)**: 60-80% of full water requirement, yield reduction < 10%
- **Regulated deficit irrigation (RDI)**: 50-60% of full water, targeted to less-sensitive growth stages, yield reduction ~5-15%
- **Seasonal drought management**: Accepting water deficit to match water availability, yield reduction variable

This approach has proven successful for:
- **Cotton**: 15-40% water savings with <5-10% yield reduction
- **Wheat**: 20-30% water savings with <10% yield reduction
- **Corn**: 20-40% water savings (hybrid-dependent) with <15% yield reduction

However, **soybean-specific data are limited**, and published results show high variability (yield reductions ranging from 5-30% for similar water deficits), suggesting regional or management-dependent effects not yet characterized.

#### C. Knowledge Gaps

1. **Regional specificity**: Most published soybean deficit irrigation research is from Australia, Brazil, or limited US locations. Multi-region US data are needed.
2. **Physiological mechanisms**: Limited understanding of which physiological processes (photosynthesis, assimilate partitioning, reproduction) are most affected by deficit stress in soybean.
3. **Model representation**: Existing process-based models (DSSAT, RZWQM, RZWQM2, AquaCrop) inadequately represent soybean physiological response to water deficit.
4. **Decision framework**: No published farmer-ready tools for deficit irrigation planning in soybean.

### Proposed Research Approach

This proposal implements a **systems-based approach** linking three complementary research components:

#### Objective 1: Field Experiments (Field Data Generation)
- Multi-location, multi-year field studies measuring soybean yield, water use efficiency, and physiological responses across four water treatments (rainfed, 25%, 50%, 100% irrigation)
- Direct measurement of soil moisture, plant water status, physiological parameters (photosynthesis, biomass accumulation)
- Establishment of region-specific yield-water response curves

#### Objectives 2-3: Model Improvements (Mechanistic Understanding)
- **Objective 2**: Improve soil-water dynamics module by incorporating deficit-induced changes in water availability and plant-available water
- **Objective 3**: Enhance crop physiology module by mechanistically representing soybean's physiological responses to water stress (stomatal conductance, photosynthesis, assimilate partitioning)

#### Objectives 4-6: Model Applications (Decision Tools)
- **Objective 4**: Use improved models for climate and management scenario analysis to identify optimal deficit irrigation strategies for each region
- **Objective 5**: Conduct regional vulnerability assessment identifying which US regions face greatest water scarcity risk and deficit irrigation potential
- **Objective 6**: Develop policy analysis examining adoption barriers, incentives, and cost-benefit tradeoffs

### Significance: Why This Research Matters

1. **Addresses a Critical Gap**: First comprehensive, multi-region, field-modeling integration for soybean deficit irrigation in the US.

2. **Timely**: Climate change is increasing frequency of droughts. This research provides actionable tools for adaptation.

3. **Broad Applicability**: Soybean is grown on >87 million US acres. Even small improvements in water efficiency translate to enormous water conservation potential.

4. **Economic Impact**: Water is an increasingly constraining input, especially in groundwater-dependent regions. Deficit irrigation can reduce pumping costs by $50-150/acre while maintaining profitability.

5. **Environmental Significance**: Groundwater depletion is unsustainable in many soybean-producing regions (High Plains aquifer is being depleted at rates of 1-3 m/year in some areas). Reducing irrigation water demand is essential for long-term sustainability.

6. **Farmer Engagement**: Direct collaboration with farmers, irrigation dealers, and extension ensures research is relevant and adoption-ready.

## III. RESEARCH OBJECTIVES AND APPROACH

### Overall Research Goals

1. Quantify soybean yield response to deficit irrigation across major US soybean regions
2. Develop improved process-based models mechanistically representing deficit irrigation effects
3. Create farmer-ready decision tools for deficit irrigation planning
4. Identify regions of highest water vulnerability and deficit irrigation potential
5. Provide policy-relevant analysis of deficit irrigation adoption barriers and incentives

### Detailed Objectives and Methods

#### OBJECTIVE 1: Field Experiments on Soybean Deficit Irrigation

**Goal**: Establish multi-location, multi-year field experiments documenting soybean yield, water use efficiency, and physiological responses across irrigation levels.

**Study Sites** (5 locations, 3 years each):
- **Nebraska (Central)**: High irrigation potential, water-limited climate
- **Iowa (Eastern)**: Moderate water stress, major soybean producer
- **Illinois (Central)**: Lower water stress, humid climate
- **Arkansas (Eastern)**: High irrigation adoption, high water stress
- **Minnesota (Southern)**: Cold region with emerging irrigation

**Experimental Design**:
- **Randomized Complete Block Design (RCBD)** with 3-4 replicates per site
- **Treatments**: Four irrigation levels reflecting regional water availability:
  - FI (Full Irrigation): 250 mm seasonal water application
  - RDI-50%: 125 mm (50% of full irrigation)
  - RDI-25%: 63 mm (25% of full irrigation)
  - Rainfed: 0 mm irrigation (baseline)

**Irrigation System**:
- Drip or subsurface drip irrigation with soil moisture sensors
- Automated scheduling based on plant-available water thresholds
- Event-based reporting (timing, amount, soil moisture status)

**Measurements**:

*Yield and WUE Components*:
- Grain yield (kg/ha)
- Water use efficiency (kg grain / mm water applied)
- Seed number and weight
- Harvest index

*Soil and Water*:
- Volumetric soil moisture (0-60 cm profile, 10 cm increments)
- Soil water tension (matric potential)
- Actual evapotranspiration (ET, via water balance)

*Plant Water Status*:
- Pre-dawn leaf water potential (ψpd) 
- Midday stomatal conductance (gs)
- Plant biomass accumulation (destructive sampling at R1, R3, R5, maturity)

*Physiological Parameters*:
- Net photosynthesis (A) at midday
- Chlorophyll content (SPAD)
- Leaf gas exchange (from R1 onward)

*Phenological Observations*:
- Growth stage progression
- Flowering date (R1), pod initiation (R3), physiological maturity (R7)

**Data Management**:
- Centralized database with real-time QA/QC
- Reproducible data processing scripts (R)
- Open-access dataset deposited in public repository

#### OBJECTIVE 2: Model Improvement - Soil-Water Dynamics Module

**Goal**: Enhance process-based model representation of soil water availability, water movement, and plant-available water dynamics under deficit irrigation.

**Approach**:

*Current Model Limitations*:
- Most models use simple water balance approaches (piecewise plant-available water)
- Inadequate representation of water redistribution in soil profile
- Poor representation of deficit-induced changes in soil water retention

*Model Improvements*:

1. **Enhanced Water Retention Curves**:
   - Implement van Genuchten water retention model for each study site
   - Calibrate with field soil samples and pressure plate measurements
   - Account for soil texture and organic matter effects

2. **Macropore Flow Representation**:
   - Add preferential flow pathways (structural cracks, root biopores) in heavy clay soils
   - Use dual-porosity approach for water movement

3. **Root Water Uptake Function**:
   - Develop dynamic root distribution based on soil water availability
   - Implement water stress reduction function linking soil water tension to uptake rate
   - Calibrate using field plant water potential measurements

4. **Model Selection and Implementation**:
   - Use RZWQM2 (Root Zone Water Quality Management model) as base framework
   - Implement improvements in C++ code
   - Validate against field measurements from Objective 1

**Validation Strategy**:
- Compare model predictions vs. observed soil moisture for each treatment
- Evaluate goodness-of-fit metrics (RMSE, NSE, PBIAS)
- Sensitivity analysis identifying parameters with greatest influence on predictions

#### OBJECTIVE 3: Model Improvement - Crop Physiology Module

**Goal**: Develop improved representation of soybean physiological response to water stress, including stomatal conductance, photosynthesis, and assimilate partitioning.

**Approach**:

*Current Model Limitations*:
- Generic water stress functions not soybean-specific
- Poor integration between water status and physiological processes
- Limited representation of reproductive-stage water stress effects

*Model Improvements*:

1. **Water Stress Index Development**:
   - Develop normalized water stress index (NWSI) combining:
     - Soil water fraction available
     - Plant water potential (measured from Objective 1)
     - Atmospheric demand (vapor pressure deficit, VPD)
   - Calibrate against observed stomatal conductance measurements

2. **Stomatal Conductance Model**:
   - Implement Leuning or Ball-Berry type model
   - Link stomatal response to soil water, VPD, and photosynthetic demand
   - Parameterize using field gas exchange measurements

3. **Photosynthesis Reduction under Stress**:
   - Model both stomatal and non-stomatal limitations
   - Link to leaf turgor and osmotic adjustment
   - Calibrate using field A-ci curves

4. **Reproductive Organ Sensitivity**:
   - Develop growth reduction functions for fruit/pod development under stress
   - Link to assimilate availability and partitioning
   - Calibrate using pod and seed development data from Objective 1

5. **Model Implementation**:
   - Couple improved physiology module with soil-water module (Objective 2)
   - Use Functional-Structural Plant Models (FSPM) approach if data warrant
   - Focus on parsimonious representation (minimize parameters) for practical use

**Parameterization Strategy**:
- Field measurements from Objective 1 provide primary data source
- Literature values as secondary sources (with sensitivity analysis)
- Cross-validation using regional experiments not included in calibration

#### OBJECTIVE 4: Model Application - Scenario Analysis and Decision Tools

**Goal**: Use improved models to simulate climate and management scenarios, identifying optimal deficit irrigation strategies for each region.

**Approach**:

1. **Climate Scenario Development**:
   - Use CMIP5 climate model ensemble (5-10 models)
   - Extract historical (1980-2015) and future projections (2040-2070)
   - Focus on precipitation and temperature (primary water availability drivers)
   - Calculate derived variables (reference ET, drought indices)

2. **Management Scenario Design**:
   - Test combinations of:
     - Irrigation levels (0-250 mm, 25 mm increments)
     - Irrigation timing (early season, mid-season, late-season emphasis)
     - Cultivar maturity groups (for regional adaptation)
     - Soil types (sandy, loam, clay)

3. **Model Simulation**:
   - Run calibrated models for each site × climate × management scenario
   - Output: Yield, water use, water use efficiency, soil water, plant water status
   - Uncertainty analysis: Use climate ensemble to quantify yield variability

4. **Decision Support Tool Development**:
   - Create user-friendly calculator for farmers/advisors
   - Inputs: Site location, soil type, water available, farmer objectives
   - Outputs: Recommended irrigation strategy, expected yield, water use, profitability
   - Platform: Web-based tool + mobile app + spreadsheet template

5. **Validation**:
   - Test tool recommendations against independent field data
   - Gather farmer feedback through focus groups and on-farm validation trials
   - Iteratively improve tool based on feedback

#### OBJECTIVE 5: Regional Vulnerability Assessment

**Goal**: Assess soybean production vulnerability to water scarcity across major US soybean regions under current and future climate scenarios.

**Approach**:

1. **Vulnerability Index Development**:
   - Integrate multiple metrics:
     - Water stress frequency (days/season with deficit)
     - Production volume at risk (acres × yield potential)
     - Groundwater depletion rates (where applicable)
     - Current irrigation infrastructure
     - Climate variability (inter-annual precipitation variability)

2. **Regional Analysis**:
   - Delineate major soybean production regions (Corn Belt, Great Plains, Delta, etc.)
   - Calculate vulnerability metrics for each region
   - Visualize results as vulnerability maps (current and future scenarios)

3. **Deficit Irrigation Potential**:
   - Estimate water savings potential if deficit irrigation adopted at scale
   - Consider infrastructure requirements (irrigation system availability)
   - Account for groundwater depletion urgency

4. **Mapping and Communication**:
   - Create interactive maps showing:
     - Current and projected vulnerability
     - Deficit irrigation adoption potential
     - Water savings estimates
   - Target audiences: USDA agencies, state agricultural departments, farm organizations

#### OBJECTIVE 6: Policy Analysis and Cost-Benefit Assessment

**Goal**: Examine adoption barriers, incentives, and economic viability of deficit irrigation for soybean farmers.

**Approach**:

1. **Cost-Benefit Analysis**:
   - Estimate costs:
     - Irrigation system installation/maintenance
     - Fuel/energy for pumping
     - Soil moisture monitoring (sensors, labor)
   - Estimate benefits:
     - Yield maintained vs. rainfed
     - Water cost avoided
     - Reduced groundwater depletion (ecosystem service value)
   - Calculate breakeven points for different farm sizes and water costs

2. **Adoption Barriers Survey**:
   - Interview 50-75 farmers across study regions
   - Identify knowledge, economic, social, and institutional barriers
   - Document farmer perceptions of water stress and adaptation interest

3. **Policy Recommendations**:
   - Review existing incentive programs (cost-share, tax credits, water pricing)
   - Identify gaps and opportunities
   - Develop policy recommendations for:
     - USDA programs (CRP, CSP, NRCS)
     - State water agencies
     - Irrigation districts

4. **Stakeholder Engagement**:
   - Present findings to farmer groups, extension educators, policymakers
   - Gather feedback on tool usefulness and adoption likelihood
   - Incorporate feedback into recommendations
